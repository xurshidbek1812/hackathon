Every traffic event on the road,
and a warning before the crash.

A fixed CCTV camera, 14 event classes, no labels. We detect and track every road user, turn trajectories into events with scene-aware rules, and score accident risk frame by frame using only the past.

01 · Approach

Problem & approach

Part A returns every event as [start_sec, end_sec, label], matched at temporal IoU 0.3 / 0.5 / 0.7. Part B returns, at every frame, the probability that an accident starts within 5 s — using only frames already seen.

learned modelhand-written logicscene knowledgeoutput
1

Detect & track

YOLO11-m (COCO, open weights) finds cars, buses, trucks, motorbikes, bicycles, people, animals and loose objects at 960 px, 10 frames per second (every 3rd frame of the 30 fps 4K video). Riders and people inside vehicles are suppressed so they never look like pedestrians. Our own ByteTrack-style tracker (two-stage IoU matching, constant-velocity prediction, timestamps not frame counts) links boxes into tracks; fragments of a parked vehicle are re-joined after occlusion.

2

Trajectories in body units

We take the bottom-centre of each box as its ground point, smooth it, and measure speed in body units per second (pixels ÷ √(w·h)). Far and near cars get comparable thresholds without camera calibration. A flow field learned from the sample videos gives each road cell its usual driving direction and marks where vehicles routinely queue.

3

Rules → segments

Each class is a small, testable rule on trajectories plus the scene layout (stop lines, crossings, solid lines, junction box) drawn by hand on a sample frame, since no camera.md came with the kit. Frame flags become segments; gaps are bridged, blips dropped and same-class overlaps merged, because boundaries are scored at IoU 0.7.

06 · Report

Technical report

What we built

A single-pass pipeline: YOLO11 detection at 10 frames per second, our own ByteTrack-style tracker, smoothed trajectories measured in body units, and one small rule per event class that combines trajectories with the fixed scene layout. Classes a COCO detector cannot see get pixel cues: signal state from the signal-head region, static foreground blobs against a median background (debris), and flickering fire-coloured regions (fire), grey growing low-texture regions (smoke). Part B runs its own causal tracker and combines time-to-collision, hard braking, swerving, wrong-way motion and pedestrians on the road into a logistic risk score with fast rise and slow decay.

How we measured

We labelled the sample videos ourselves with our labeling tool, following the task's start/end conventions, and scored every change with the official evaluate.py. On all four sample videos (C3896, C3897, C3902, C3905), Score A went from 0.04 for the first version to 0.42:

Solid-line crossing0.17lane dividers solid only from the gantry to the stop line
Not yielding to a pedestrian0.05still the weakest class: short events, hard to time

Classes we predicted but that were never labelled (accident, U-turn) each add a zero to the macro average, so much of the gain came from removing those false alarms. Four videos are a small dev set: we kept only fixes that follow from a traffic rule, not sweeps that won by a single event.

What worked

  • Body-unit speeds. Dividing pixel motion by box size made one set of thresholds work across the depth of the image.
  • Looking at every candidate. With no labels, we rendered each rule's candidate events on the frame and inspected them. The first full run on the 4K sample gave 86 events for 5½ minutes of ordinary traffic; the fixes below brought it to 13 plausible ones.
  • Signal-free red light. The main approach's signal heads face away from the camera, so the red phase is inferred from the queue: at least two vehicles standing at the stop line for 5 s and nothing crossing it.
  • Camera alignment. The "fixed" camera was nudged between recordings by up to 5.7 % of the frame — a lane width at the stop line. Each video is registered to our reference view (SIFT + RANSAC homography) and the whole scene layout is moved onto it.
  • Overlapping decode and inference. 4K H.264 decoding dominates the runtime; a reader thread (Part A) and a one-sample-lag detector worker (Part B) cut the harness time from 973 s to 549 s on a laptop GPU (budget 1021 s).

What did not work (and what we changed)

  • fp16 on a GTX 1650 Ti returned NaN boxes — our first run found zero tracks. Half precision is now disabled on GTX 16xx cards.
  • Identity switches in dense queues produced fake U-turns and near misses. Stricter IoU gating, splitting tracks at physically impossible jumps and dropping boxes cut by the frame edge fixed most of them.
  • Image-plane time-to-collision flags platoons, merges and opposite carriageways (perspective squeezes the median). Near misses now require crossing paths held for 0.3 s by road users large enough to judge.
  • The learned road mask spilled onto the pavement at the bus stop, turning waiting passengers into jaywalkers; we drew the carriageway by hand.
  • Accidents from rules alone are brittle. On 40 real crash clips (ACCIDENT dataset) our first rule caught none: it wanted 2 s of contact, but tracks break at impact. Judging approach, impact and halt instead finds 10 of 40 with no false alarms on our videos.
  • Crash anticipation from trajectories did not work. On the same 40 clips no Part B cue separates the 5 s before a crash from normal traffic, so we kept the risk model conservative: it never alarms on normal traffic.

What we would do next

  • Fine-tune a video classifier (e.g. VideoMAE) on CCD / DoTA to re-score accident and near-miss candidates.
  • Estimate a ground-plane homography from lane markings to get metric speeds and true time-to-collision.
  • Fit the Part B weights with logistic regression on our own dev labels instead of hand-set weights.

Reproducibility

Fixed seeds, deterministic tracker and rules, no internet at inference, weights in the repository. Frame skipping only kicks in as an emergency if a machine runs at more than twice the time budget.
